# What To Say When You Blank

The survival kit. Read it once now, then come back to it on the day it happens — because it will happen, and having a plan is the difference between a two-second gap and a conversation you avoid for a month afterwards.

---

## First, the thing nobody tells you

**Blanking is not an English problem.** You blank in your first language too — you lose names, you lose the word for a thing you own, you say "the whatsit".

The difference is that in your first language you do not treat it as evidence of anything. You say "what's it called" and carry on. In English you treat the same event as proof you cannot speak.

Same event. Different verdict. The verdict is the part that is wrong.

---

## The four blanks

Different problems, different fixes. Work out which one you are having.

### Blank 1 — The word is gone

You know exactly what you mean. The word will not come.

**Do not** stop, apologise, or switch languages.
**Do** describe it. This is Day 10's skill, and it works every time.

> *"It's the thing you use to open bottles."*
> *"A kind of small tool — metal, fits in your hand."*
> *"Like a knife, but not sharp."*

Say **"the word's gone —"** and then describe. Naming the problem out loud takes the panic out of it.

Nine times out of ten the other person supplies the word and the conversation continues. That is not embarrassing; that is how conversation works.

### Blank 2 — The sentence collapsed

You started a sentence and it went somewhere you cannot finish.

**Do not** restart from the beginning.
**Do** abandon it and restate.

> *"Let me rephrase that."*
> *"What I mean is —"*
> *"Sorry, that came out wrong. The point is —"*

Then say the simple version. The collapsed sentence was almost certainly too complicated. Say it in two short sentences instead.

### Blank 3 — The thread is gone

You know you were making a point. You have no idea what it was.

**Do not** go silent while hunting for it.
**Do** say so and buy time.

> *"I've lost my thread — give me a second."*
> *"Where was I? Right —"*
> *"Anyway, the point is —"*

**"Anyway, the point is —"** is the most useful of these. It forces you to produce a conclusion, and the conclusion is usually still there even when the route to it is not.

If it genuinely will not come, hand the turn over: *"Sorry — what were you asking again?"* This works and costs you nothing.

### Blank 4 — Total freeze

Nothing. No words, in any language. Chest tight, mind blank.

**Do not** try to force a sentence out.
**Do** buy time physically first.

1. **Breathe out.** Fully. Freezing usually comes with holding your breath, and the tightness makes it worse.
2. **Say one of these**, slowly:
   > *"Give me a moment."*
   > *"Let me think about how to say this."*
3. **Start with something concrete.** Not the difficult idea — something you can see or something factual. *"So — we've got about three weeks."* Concrete restarts the engine; abstract does not.

Three seconds of silence feels like a minute from the inside and passes almost unnoticed from the outside.

## Fillers: which ones to keep

Not all filler is bad. There are two kinds.

**Searching noise** — *ummm, aaah, how to say.* These signal struggle. Day 4 exists to replace these with silence.

**Discourse markers** — *well, so, right, I mean, actually, look.* These are real English and native speakers use them constantly. They mark a turn, buy a beat, and sound completely normal.

> *"Well, it depends."* — fine
> *"Ummm… it depends."* — same gap, worse signal

Swap the first kind for the second, or for silence. You are not removing the pause. You are changing what you fill it with.

---

## The two-second rule

A silent pause under two seconds is invisible. Nobody notices it. Nobody thinks anything of it.

Over about four seconds, people start to wonder — and that is when they jump in or ask if you are alright.

So you have roughly **two full seconds of free silence** after any question. Use them. Do not fill them with noise, and do not panic through them. That is the gap experienced speakers use to choose their opening.

---

## After it happens

The most damaging part of blanking is not the blank. It is the twenty minutes afterwards spent replaying it, and the conclusion that you cannot speak English.

Three things to hold onto:

**They noticed far less than you did.** You experienced it as an event. They experienced a short pause, if they registered it at all.

**Blanking is not correlated with ability.** Tired, distracted and nervous people blank. So do fluent ones, in their own language.

**One blank does not predict the next.** Do not withdraw from the conversation to protect yourself. Withdrawing is what turns one bad moment into a lasting habit of not speaking.

---

## The emergency card

If you take one thing from this file:

> **The word's gone** → describe it
> **The sentence broke** → *"let me rephrase that"*
> **The thread's gone** → *"anyway, the point is —"*
> **Total freeze** → breathe out, *"give me a moment"*, start with something concrete

Say those four responses aloud five times each, once, today. Then they are available on the day you need them — which is the only day they matter.
